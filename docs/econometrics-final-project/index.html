Econometrics Term Paper

The Effect of Fast Food Consumption on Obesity Rates Across United States

A cross-sectional OLS regression on 50 U.S. states examining what drives differences in adult obesity rates.

Carolina Varanko ECON 400, Econometrics Spring 2026
01
The Question

Research Question

Obesity is one of the most pressing public health challenges in the United States. As of 2021, over one third of American adults were classified as obese, with rates varying considerably across states. Obesity-related medical costs are estimated at over $170 billion annually.

What state-level factors explain variation in adult obesity rates, and does fast food density hold up as a driver once income, inactivity, diabetes, and urbanicity are controlled for?

Understanding these determinants is relevant not only for public health policy but also for businesses in the food service and healthcare industries, which operate under increasing regulatory scrutiny as obesity-related costs continue to grow.

02
Data & Methods

Sources, Variables & Model

The analysis draws on four public government data sources covering 50 states plus D.C. (Florida excluded due to insufficient 2021 BRFSS sample size).

Variables

  • ObesityRateAdult obesity rate (%), dependent variable. CDC BRFSS, 2021
  • FastFoodDensityFast food establishments per person. USDA Food Environment Atlas, 2020
  • MedianHouseholdIncomeMedian household income ($). Census ACS 2023, Table S1901
  • PhysicalInactivityShare of adults reporting no leisure-time physical activity (%). CDC USDSS, 2021
  • DiabetesRateAdult diabetes prevalence (%). CDC USDSS, 2021
  • UrbanBinary dummy, metro vs. non-metro. USDA Food Environment Atlas
  • FFD_IncomeInteraction term: FastFoodDensity × MedianHouseholdIncome

Model

A cross-sectional OLS regression is used: each state is one observation, and obesity rate is regressed on the six variables above simultaneously. In plain terms, the model asks how much obesity rates move with each factor while holding the other factors fixed.

ObesityRate = β0 + β1(FastFoodDensity) + β2(MedianHouseholdIncome) + β3(PhysicalInactivity) + β4(DiabetesRate) + β5(Urban) + β6(FFD_Income) + ε

The p column is the odds that a variable's apparent effect is just random noise in this particular sample of 50 states, rather than a real pattern. A p-value of 0.05 means a 5% chance the result is a fluke. The convention is: below 0.05 counts as "statistically significant," meaning the evidence is strong enough to trust.

In this table, MedianHouseholdIncome has p = 0.0129 (below 0.05), so it clears the bar. FastFoodDensity has p = 0.8684, an 87% chance its measured relationship with obesity is just noise, which is why it is not treated as a real effect here. That does not prove fast food is irrelevant; it means this data does not provide strong enough evidence that it matters once the other variables are controlled for.

The asterisks are shorthand for the same idea: *** means p < 0.001, ** means p < 0.01, * means p < 0.05.

Only MedianHouseholdIncome is significant: a $1,000 increase in median income is associated with a 0.20 percentage point decrease in obesity rate, holding all else constant.

Robustness Checks

Testing the Model

Breusch-Pagan Test

BP = 7.184, df = 6, p = 0.3042

Fail to reject H0, no heteroskedasticity detected.

Some regressions quietly let a few noisy states distort the results, making the reported error margins untrustworthy. This test checks for exactly that pattern, called heteroskedasticity. Since it passed (p = 0.30, above 0.05), the model's standard errors, and the significance results built on them, can be taken at face value.

HC1 Robust SEs

Applied as a precaution despite passing BP test.

MedianHouseholdIncome strengthens to p = 0.001 (1% level); all else unchanged.

Even though the model passed the heteroskedasticity test, robust standard errors are applied as a safety net: they recompute the error margins in a way that stays valid even if the earlier test missed something. Here, the income finding gets stronger under this stricter check, not weaker, which is a good sign that it is not a fragile result.

When two predictor variables move together too closely, like PhysicalInactivity and DiabetesRate here (r = 0.821), the regression cannot fully tell their individual effects apart, and both end up looking weaker or less significant than they really are. VIF measures how much each variable overlaps with the others; a value above 10 signals a serious problem. The highest value here, PhysicalInactivity at 4.68, reflects that overlap but stays well under the danger threshold, so the coefficient estimates remain usable.

08
Limitations

Study Limitations

  1. Florida is excluded due to insufficient 2021 BRFSS sample size.
  2. Fast food density for Maine, New York, and Texas uses county-level averages, since state-level values were suppressed in the source data.
  3. Data sources span different years: income (2023), fast food density (2020), health variables (2021).
  4. The cross-sectional design does not support causal inference. Unmeasured confounders such as cultural diet patterns and state health investment are possible.
09
Conclusions

Conclusions & Policy

Median household income is the only individually significant predictor. Higher-income states consistently exhibit lower obesity rates. This finding holds under robust standard errors.

Income-related interventions, such as expanding access to affordable healthy food in lower-income states and subsidizing healthier food alternatives, are likely to have the greatest impact on reducing obesity. Fast food density alone is not a reliable lever once other factors are controlled for.
